Research questions

Resistive memory

Can the motion of one metal atom change a memory cell’s resistance?

In monolayer molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂), I compared how gold, silver, and copper atoms leave an electrode, move across the surface, and bind at a sulfur vacancy. My ongoing work in hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) asks how the type of vacancy and the gap between the metal and the layer change those pathways.

Read the MoS₂ study

Magnetic memory

Which defects make a magnetic state easier to read?

In a magnetic tunnel junction, current depends on whether the magnetizations of the two electrodes are parallel or antiparallel. I plan to test how a vacancy or a metal atom in an h-BN barrier changes the difference between those currents.

Ferroelectric and phase-change memory

How can we tell a polarization reversal from a change of crystal phase?

In collaborative work on indium selenide (In₂Se₃), we found that encapsulation helped a crystalline phase remain stable in air. I want to learn how contacts and defects stabilize different states and how the electrical readout changes when the crystal changes phase or its polarization reverses.

Read the In₂Se₃ study

Energy storage

Where can a layered material store ions and still let them move?

My earlier collaborative work on niobium-doped MXenes examined how composition affects charge storage. I want to predict where ions bind in a layered electrode and how easily they move between those sites during charging and discharging.

Research goals

I want a calculation to tell us which material to make, which interface to change, or which measurement would distinguish two explanations.

Identify the atomic origin of a measured signal

I connect electronic structure, migration barriers, and quantum transport. In a study of gold/h-BN/gold devices, our transport calculations showed how changing the electrode–h-BN separation can explain large variations in current.

Read the transport study

Find materials and interfaces worth making

Screen defects, metal contacts, and layer combinations for a specific function, such as trapping a metal atom strongly enough to form a memory state while still allowing it to leave during reset.

Put predictions to an experimental test

Work with experimental collaborators to vary one feature, such as electrode metal or contact spacing. Compare the predicted and measured trends, then use the differences to refine the model and choose the next experiment.
